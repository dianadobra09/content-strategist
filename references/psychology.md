# Feelings in posts

Every post makes the reader feel something. The method comes from a LinkedIn creator's article on why posts spread (`references/reach.md`): a post spreads when it makes people feel something strongly enough to react. The article shows no data for it, so compare results in `outputs/log.md`. The feeling carries the substance (`CLAUDE.md` rule 2) and stays true (rule 1).

## On every draft

1. Pick the feeling the first line creates and one the body adds. Write both in the brief: `Feeling: recognition → discomfort with a way out`. All six at once confuses the reader.
2. The first line creates the first feeling at once, in the author's words: recognition opens on the moment the reader knows, discomfort on the uncomfortable fact, curiosity on the gap.
3. The body adds the second feeling and the substance.
4. End with something the reader can do: the author's real question, or a step they can use.
5. Check: is it true? Would the author say it in person? Does the post earn the feeling? Does it serve the reader, or the author? A post that fails one goes back.

## The six feelings

The examples come from the posts in `examples/outputs/posts/published/linkedin/`. The author's wording rules still apply (see "In the author's words").

### 1. Recognition: "that's me"
Say what the reader has thought but never said, past the surface.
Example: "think about the interviews or support tickets that you paste into ChatGPT to decide what to build next." (02)
Not this: telling readers what they feel.

### 2. Looking smart by sharing
People share what makes them look informed: insider knowledge, an early contrarian take, a tactical breakdown, data or research.
Example: the four-step "how it works:" list on how a model picks the most likely answer (01); the court filings behind a bankruptcy (03).
Not this: a number without its primary source.

### 3. Belonging
Say who it's for, in the first words, and put the author in the group when it's true: "if English isn't your first language...".
Not this: mocking the other group.

### 4. Discomfort with a way out
Show the gap between what the reader believes and what's true, and that it can be closed.
Example: "your AI summary tends to miss important customer feedback." → the three steps to ask for instead (02).
Not this: shaming the reader, doom the author doesn't hold.

### 5. Curiosity
Specific beats general: a specific number or outcome, insider knowledge, the gap between what the reader expects and what happens.
Example: "in 2024, Google paid $2.7B to get 2 people back. the company they left behind is now bankrupt with $57M in cash." (03)
Not this: holding the answer back, a question the post then answers.

### 6. Believable hope
The outcome, the time and the method all have to feel within reach, with real proof.
Example: the author's own small, real result, with its number and period.
Not this: big or invented results.

## Two feelings together

Curiosity and discomfort, or curiosity and the fear of being in the group that fails. Use a percentage only when it's real.
Example: "we're trying to get the design team to replace 80% of their Figma with a React repo." (curiosity and recognition, 04)

## OK ragebait: changing a belief

Not anger. It challenges something the reader believes, so the reader either argues or changes their mind:

1. State the belief the reader really holds.
2. Create doubt with the author's own evidence.
3. Give the new frame: what the author thinks.
4. Show what it means for the reader.
5. Offer a way forward (optional): the author's tip, or the product, named once.

Example: "using AI is making us basic." → how models pick the most likely answer → the studies → asking for five answers (01). It argues with an idea, never a person. No invented "I know founders who...", no "They're wrong".

## In the author's words

Many viral examples lean on "You're not X. You're Y." and "This isn't X. This is Y.", the strongest AI tell in `humanizer`, so the kill list keeps them out. The same feeling works as a plain sentence that names the real cause.

## Left out

"Comment 'WORD' and I'll send it" posts and stacked FOMO lines. LinkedIn says it doesn't promote posts that ask for engagement, and social proof would have to be real. A real reason to act is fine.
